Why we track this

A history of exclusion, repeal, and congressional regret

The Chinese Exclusion Act made Chinese identity a basis for federal restrictions on immigration, reentry, and naturalization. More than a century later, Congress formally acknowledged and regretted the exclusion laws and their effects.

Historical measures provide context only. They are not included in the dashboard’s tracker totals.

Historical review

A focused account of one flagship law

This page is not a catalog of every law affecting people of Chinese origin. It centers the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, places it within the exclusion regime it came to symbolize, and examines the significance of Congress’s formal expressions of regret in 2011 and 2012.

Historical setting

From immigration restriction to unequal civic membership

In the late nineteenth century, federal policy increasingly singled out Chinese people for restrictions that extended beyond admission at the border. The 1882 Act became the central statute in a broader legal regime governing whether Chinese laborers could enter, whether lawful residents could return after travel, and whether Chinese people could become U.S. citizens through naturalization.

That combination made exclusion a civil-rights issue as well as an immigration issue. Legal rules based on Chinese identity shaped mobility, residence, access to citizenship, and participation in national life.

  1. 1882

    Chinese Exclusion Act

    The flagship federal exclusion law

    The Act suspended the immigration of Chinese laborers for ten years, required specified documentation for Chinese laborers seeking to leave and return, and prohibited state and federal courts from admitting Chinese people to citizenship. Its reentry provisions therefore reached Chinese laborers already residing lawfully in the United States, not only prospective immigrants.

    Congress later amended, extended, and intensified the exclusion regime. The 1882 Act remains the focal point here because it became the defining symbol of that system and was specifically named in both chambers’ later resolutions of regret.

    View the act at the National Archives
  2. 1943

    Repeal

    The exclusion laws end, but the history remains

    Congress repealed the Chinese exclusion laws in 1943 and again permitted Chinese immigrants to naturalize. Repeal ended the formal exclusion regime; it did not erase the decades in which federal law had treated Chinese identity as a basis for unequal rights and restrictions.

  3. 2011–12

    Formal congressional regret

    Congress names the harm and expresses regret

    The Senate agreed to S. Res. 201 in 2011, and the House unanimously adopted H. Res. 683 on June 18, 2012. The resolutions reviewed the history of federal discrimination against Chinese people and formally expressed regret for the exclusion laws.

    The House resolution stated that the legislation “adversely affected Chinese persons in the United States and limited their civil rights.” Its final text regretted laws that adversely affected people of Chinese origin “because of their ethnicity.” The Senate resolution likewise described discriminatory laws and acknowledged that the exclusion regime was incompatible with the nation’s founding principles.

    The resolutions were acts of institutional acknowledgment, not new civil remedies. The House resolution expressly disclaimed creating a legal claim or right to compensation. Their importance here is historical: Congress placed exclusion within a record of unequal treatment and formally accepted responsibility for remembering it.

Civil-rights significance

Why this history informs the present tracker

Civil rights concern the terms on which people are treated by law and allowed to participate in civic life. The exclusion era shows how a nationality- or ethnicity-based classification can operate through several legal mechanisms at once: entry and reentry rules, documentation burdens, access to naturalization, enforcement procedures, and distinctions between residents and citizens.

This history does not mean that every current proposal is equivalent to the Chinese Exclusion Act. It supplies a disciplined set of questions: What classification does the text use? Which individuals can it reach? Are citizens or lawful permanent residents exempt? What right, opportunity, or legal interest is restricted? What procedures and penalties enforce the distinction?

The tracker answers those questions from the text of each measure and keeps historical interpretation separate from current bill counts.
